# Get Paid in 14 Days: The Zero-Dollar Invoicing and Collections System

A free-tier billing system that fixes the real cause of late payments, which is your process rather than your software.

## Start here: the software is not the expensive part

- Sending invoices is free almost everywhere in 2026. Moving the money is what costs you.
- Reference rates commonly published in 2026: Wave charges about 2.9% plus 0.60 USD per card payment and 1% for bank transfer with a 1 USD minimum. Square Invoices is commonly listed at 3.3% plus 0.30 USD per online card payment and 1% for bank transfer with a per transaction cap. PayPal Invoicing is commonly listed at 2.99% plus 0.49 USD per card. Stripe Invoicing adds roughly 0.4% to 0.5% on top of standard processing.
- Run the math before you choose. On a 5,000 USD invoice, a 2.9% plus 0.30 USD card fee is roughly 145 USD. The same invoice paid by capped bank transfer is often under 20 USD.
- Conclusion: choose your payment rails by invoice size, not by which dashboard you find prettiest.

## The free tiers, and the caps that actually bite

Vendors change these numbers often and third party roundups contradict each other, so treat the list below as a starting point and confirm on the vendor pricing page before you commit.

- Wave Starter: unlimited invoices and clients plus basic double entry accounting. Common watch out is that recurring invoicing and reduced card fees sit on the paid tier.
- Zoho Invoice: the richest free feature set, including a client portal, time tracking, recurring billing and multi currency. Reported caps vary a lot by source and region, from a five client limit to a 500 invoice per year limit to an annual revenue threshold. Assume a cap exists and find yours.
- Invoice Ninja: a free cloud tier with a client cap that sources report as either 5 or 20, plus vendor branding. The self hosted version is genuinely unlimited if you can run a small server.
- Square Invoices: no monthly fee and unlimited invoices, strongest if you already sell in person. Card rate is higher than average.
- PayPal Invoicing: no monthly fee and the highest client recognition, which reduces payment hesitation.

One rule regardless of choice: export a backup every month. Never let a free tool hold the only copy of your billing history, and note that at least one popular free tier is reported to purge data after a long period of inactivity.

## Why invoices go late, and it is rarely the tool

- Intuit published a 2025 small business late payments report finding that US small businesses were owed roughly 17,500 USD on average in unpaid invoices, that a majority were waiting on overdue money, and that close to half reported invoices more than 30 days late.
- Late payment is a queue problem. Your invoice sits in an accounts payable queue behind vendors who made themselves easier to pay.
- Four failure points cause most of it: the invoice reached the wrong person, it lacked a purchase order or reference number, the terms were never agreed in advance, and nobody followed up on a schedule.

## Design the terms before you do the work

- Put payment terms in the proposal, never for the first time on the invoice. The invoice should contain zero surprises.
- Default to a 50% deposit on project work, and full payment up front below a threshold you set. Many solo operators use 1,000 USD as that line.
- Prefer Net 14 or due on receipt over Net 30 unless client procurement policy forces otherwise. Shorter stated terms shift the whole payment distribution earlier even when clients slip.
- Name a late fee, for example 1.5% per month, plus a clause that pauses new work. You may never charge it. It changes behavior because it signals that you track this.
- Ask one question before the first invoice: who should receive invoices, and do you need a purchase order number or a vendor onboarding form. That single question removes the most common two week delay.

## The billing hour

Block one hour per week, same day and same time.

1. Invoice everything you delivered. Aging starts the day you send, not the day you finish.
2. Review the aging list in four buckets: 0 to 14 days, 15 to 30, 31 to 60, and over 60.
3. Send the scheduled nudges described below.
4. Log every promise to pay date and set a reminder for the day after it.
5. Reconcile payments received and mark them paid so nobody gets chased twice.

## The four touch chase cadence

Polite, scheduled and unemotional. Copy the structure and write it in your own voice.

- Day zero, on send: put the invoice number and the amount in the subject line. Include a payment link in the email body and restate the due date in the first sentence.
- Three days before due: one line confirming the invoice is in their system and scheduled.
- One day after due: reply inside the same email thread. State the amount and due date, then ask one question, which is whether they can confirm the payment run date.
- Seven days after due: add the accounts payable contact to the thread and ask for a specific date rather than a status.
- Twenty one days after due: pause new work in writing, restate the late fee, and offer a payment plan.

Escalate the channel, not the tone. Email, then a phone call, then a formal letter.

## Make paying you effortless

- One click beats everything. A payment link in the email body outperforms a PDF with bank details buried in the footer.
- Offer at least two rails: card for speed on small invoices, bank transfer for large ones.
- Put your legal entity name, tax registration number and address on every invoice. Procurement teams reject invoices missing these far more often than founders expect.
- Number invoices sequentially with no gaps, which makes disputes and bookkeeping trivial.
- Send from a domain email address rather than a free mailbox.
- Write line items in the same language as the contract or purchase order so approvers can match them without asking you.

## Zero dollar guardrails

- Export invoices and client records monthly to storage you control.
- Track one number by hand: average days to pay, calculated as paid date minus sent date. If it rises two months in a row, tighten your terms rather than blaming clients.
- Never accept card on a very large invoice without also offering bank transfer.
- Set a calendar reminder before any free tier reset, invoice count rollover or inactivity purge.
- Keep a two page agreement covering scope, terms and late fees. A free template is fine, as consistency matters more than legal polish at this stage.

## How to use

Work through this in one sitting, then run it weekly.

1. Pick one free invoicing tool today based on how your clients prefer to pay, not on feature lists. If you have no strong preference, start with the option that bundles basic accounting.
2. Open your vendor pricing page and write down three numbers on a sticky note: your card rate, your bank transfer rate, and your free tier cap. These are the only numbers that matter.
3. Rewrite your proposal template tonight to include deposit, terms, late fee and the accounts payable question.
4. Set a recurring weekly calendar block called Billing Hour and protect it like a client meeting.
5. Draft the five chase emails once, save them as templates, and never write them from scratch again.
6. Calculate your current average days to pay from the last ten invoices. That is your baseline.
7. Re run the baseline in 30 days. If the number did not fall, the cadence is not being followed, not that the system failed.

Everything above runs on free tiers. The only real cost is the discipline of one protected hour each week.
